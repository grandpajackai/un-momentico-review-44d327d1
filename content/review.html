Hola, and welcome.

This is the first note from Un Momentico. If you're reading it, you either know me, or someone thought you might need a pause. Either way, I'm grateful you're here.

In a Cuban home, when someone says "un momentico," you stop what you're doing. It never meant "just a second." It meant: sit down, look at me, be here. That's what I want these notes to be. A few quiet minutes in a week that probably hasn't been quiet.

Each issue will have three small things: something about how this work actually happens, one question to sit with, and a word for anyone who's carrying something heavy.

What a first session actually feels like

People tell me they were nervous before their first session. They didn't know what to say, or whether they had to believe something to come in.

Here's the truth: you don't have to prepare anything.

We slow down. You talk, I listen. Some people come with one clear question. Some come with a feeling they can't name yet. Both are welcome. There's no script, and there's nothing to perform.

I'm open about the spirit, energy and faith that guide my own life. That's my story. It isn't a requirement for yours. People of every faith, and of none, sit with me. The only thing I ask is that you arrive open.

Sessions are scheduled for one hour, and I always leave extra time after. I don't believe in cutting someone off in the middle of something important. Your session ends when you're ready, not when the clock says.

Most people leave with one thing to sit with, and one small step for the week. Not ten. One.

This week's question

Where in my life am I staying busy so I don't have to sit with what my heart already knows?

Try this: find five quiet minutes. Phone face down. Three slow breaths. Read the question twice, then write whatever comes first, without editing it.

Then ask a second, smaller question: Am I moving from fear, or from love?

You don't have to act on everything you write. Pick one honest step and take it before the week is over. Discern, then proceed with more light and love.

For anyone carrying grief

If someone you love has passed, I want you to hear this clearly: you don't have to be "over it" to keep living, and you don't have to carry it alone.

Grief doesn't follow a timeline. It shows up in a song at the grocery store, or on a Tuesday that should have been ordinary. That's not you doing grief wrong. That's love with nowhere to go.

In some sessions I offer medium guidance: a respectful opening to receive messages from loved ones who have passed, when that gift is present. I hold it with care and humility, and I never promise a specific message or outcome. Sometimes the most important part of the hour is simply talking about the person: their laugh, their habits, the way they said your name.

If that's where you are, this space is open to you. And please keep leaning on your doctor, your counselor and your people too. This work sits alongside them, not in place of them.

Until next week

Reply to this email and tell me what you'd like me to write about next. I read every reply.

Blessings and love, Danny

Blog 1 — What a first session actually feels like

Status: Full draft for review. Brandon liked the topic. Not published. Pillar: What a session is (and isn't) Suggested URL: /journal/what-a-first-session-feels-like/ Meta title (≤60): What a First Session Feels Like | Un Momentico Meta description (≤155): Nervous about a first spiritual guidance session? Here's what actually happens with Danny Cabrera: no script, no rush, and a session that ends when you're ready. Length: ~1,050 words

The short answer

A first session with me is a calm, one-on-one conversation. It's scheduled for one hour, with extra time built in afterwards. You don't need to prepare anything or share my faith. We slow down, you talk, I listen, and you leave with one honest thing to sit with. Your session ends when you're ready, not when the clock says.

"I didn't know what to say"

Almost everyone tells me some version of this after their first session: "I was nervous. I didn't know what I was supposed to say."

I understand. Most of us have never sat with someone whose only job, for an hour, is to listen to us. We're used to appointments with a clipboard, a timer and a next patient waiting in the hall. So it makes sense to walk in braced for that.

That's not what happens here. Let me walk you through it, so the only surprise is how unhurried it feels.

Before you arrive

You don't have to prepare anything. Really.

Some people come with a clear question: a decision at work, a relationship, a move, a sense that their life isn't lined up with what they're meant to do. Others come with something they can't name yet, just a heaviness or a restlessness. Both are completely welcome. Sometimes the real question shows up halfway through the hour.

You also don't need to believe what I believe. I'm open about the spirit, energy and faith that guide my own life. That's my story and my motivation. It isn't a test you have to pass. People of every faith, and of none, sit with me. The only thing I ask is that you arrive open.

Sessions can happen in English or in Spanish, or in the mix of both that a lot of us in South Florida actually speak at home.

The first ten minutes

We start by slowing down. That's it. No forms to fill out in front of me, no speech about my credentials.

I'll ask what's on your heart, and then I'll mostly be quiet. You can start anywhere. If you start in the middle, that's fine. If you start with "I don't know why I'm here," that's often the most honest beginning there is.

Most people let out a breath in those first minutes that they didn't know they were holding. That's usually when the real conversation begins.

The middle of the hour

This part is different for everyone, because it follows you.

For some people it's practical: we look at the gifts already placed in them, what people already come to them for, what leaves them at peace afterwards (not just excited during), and what one honest next step would look like.

For some people it's grief. We talk about someone they've lost: who they were, how they laughed, what's been left unsaid. In some sessions I offer medium guidance, a respectful opening to receive messages from loved ones who have passed, when that gift is present. I hold that part with care and humility, and I'm always clear about one thing: I never promise a specific message or outcome. When something comes, I share it gently and you tell me what, if anything, it means to you. When nothing comes, we still sit with what's on your heart.

For many people it's a bit of both.

What it is, and what it isn't

It helps to be clear about this from the start.

A session is: a real conversation; a pause to discern; spiritual and personal insight; a confidential space, in English or Spanish.

A session isn't: a diagnosis or therapy; medical, psychological, legal or financial advice; a performance; a promise of a specific message; a race against the clock.

If you're working with a doctor, a counselor or a therapist, please keep doing that. This work can sit alongside them. It doesn't replace them.

Why the session doesn't end on the hour

Sessions are scheduled for one hour. But I always leave buffer time afterwards, on purpose.

Here's why. The most important thing someone says in a session very often comes in the last few minutes, right when they'd normally be glancing at the clock. I don't want anyone walked out the door in the middle of a breakthrough because of a calendar.

So I'll say it plainly: your session ends when you're ready, not when the clock says.

What you leave with

Not a ten-step plan. Usually one thing to sit with (a question, a word, a feeling worth paying attention to) and one small step you can actually take this week.

There's no pressure to book another session before you leave. If the hour helped, you'll know. If you'd like to come back, the door is open.

A few questions people ask

Do I have to be religious? No. My faith is my personal story. People of every faith, and of none, are welcome. Just arrive open.

What if I cry? Then you cry. There's no rush, and that's okay.

Can I bring someone with me? Families and loved ones sometimes come together. Mention it when you reach out and we'll talk about what would help most.

Will I definitely hear from someone who has passed? No one can promise that, and I won't. What I can promise is a careful, unhurried space to listen.

Whenever you're ready

If you've been curious but nervous, that's okay. Save this for the day you're ready, or send it to someone who's been wondering what a session is like.

Blessings and love, Danny

What to expect from a medium reading

Direct answer: A medium reading is a calm, one-on-one conversation in which the medium shares impressions they sense from loved ones who have passed (names, memories, personality traits or feelings), and you say whether they mean anything to you. A first reading usually lasts about an hour. No honest medium can promise that a specific person will come through. A good reading feels unhurried, respects your boundaries, and leaves you with comfort or clarity, not pressure.

That's the short version. Below is what it actually looks like when you sit with me, Danny Cabrera, at Un Momentico in South Florida, and how to prepare so the hour serves you.

What happens during a medium reading, step by step

Every medium works a little differently. Here's how a session with me usually unfolds.

  1. We slow down. We start with a few quiet minutes. You don't need to explain your whole story, and I'd actually rather you didn't, at first.
  2. I share what comes. Impressions can arrive as a name or initial, a memory, a personality trait, a feeling, or an image. I share them carefully and as they are, without dressing them up.
  3. You respond honestly. A simple "yes," "no" or "I'm not sure" is perfect. You never have to make something fit.
  4. We talk about what it means for you now. Often the most important part isn't the message itself. It's how you carry the love forward and how you live now.
  5. We close without rushing. Sessions are scheduled for one hour, with buffer time afterwards. Your session ends when you're ready, not when the clock says.

How long does a medium reading last?

Most first readings run 30 to 60 minutes, depending on the medium. Mine are scheduled for one hour, and I always leave extra time so nothing important gets cut off at the end.

How to prepare for your first medium reading

  • Come with an open heart, not a script. You don't need a list of tests.
  • Hold one or two open questions. For example: "Is there anything someone wants me to know?" or "What do I need to hear right now?" Open questions work better than yes/no ones.
  • Don't over-share at the start. Letting impressions come first makes them easier to weigh honestly.
  • Decide your boundaries. You can say at any point that you'd rather not talk about something, or that you need a pause.
  • Bring tissues, and patience with yourself. Readings can be emotional, in both directions.

What a medium can't promise you

This is the part many guides skip, and it matters most.

  • No one can guarantee that a specific person will come through. If someone promises that, be careful.
  • A reading isn't proof, and it isn't a prediction. It's an experience of connection that you weigh for yourself.
  • It isn't a replacement for care. A reading offers spiritual and personal insight. It isn't a substitute for medical, psychological, legal or financial advice, and it shouldn't replace your doctor, counselor or grief support.

At Un Momentico, I say this out loud before we start: I never promise a specific message or outcome. When something comes, I share it with humility. When nothing comes, we still sit with what's on your heart.

Do you have to believe in mediums, or share a faith?

No. I'm a man of faith, and I'm open about the spirit, energy and faith that guide me. But that's my story, not a requirement for yours. People of every faith, and of none, come to sit with me. The only thing that really helps is arriving open.

What if the person you hoped for doesn't come through?

It happens, and it doesn't mean you did anything wrong or that they're "not there." Sometimes someone else comes forward. Sometimes the hour becomes about the grief itself: talking about the person, what was left unsaid, and how to keep loving them while you keep living. People often tell me that part was what they needed most.

How to connect with a loved one who passed, outside of a reading

You don't need a medium to keep a relationship with someone you've lost. A few gentle practices:

  • Talk to them. Out loud, in a journal, at their favorite place.
  • Keep one ritual. Their coffee order on their birthday, their song on Sunday mornings.
  • Notice without forcing. Many people notice small signs: a song, a scent, a dream. Let them be comfort, not a test.
  • Take un momentico. Phone face down, three slow breaths, and ask: What would they want for me today?

Frequently asked questions

What should I ask a medium on my first reading? Keep it open: "Is anyone trying to connect with me?" or "What do I most need to hear right now?" Open questions leave room for what matters.

Should I tell the medium who I want to hear from? It's usually best not to at the start. Let impressions come first, then share as much as you're comfortable with.

Is a medium reading the same as a psychic reading? Not exactly. Mediumship focuses on connection with loved ones who have passed. Psychic readings usually focus on the person in front of the reader. My sessions also include spiritual guidance and life-purpose coaching.

Can a medium reading help with grief? Many people find comfort and a sense of connection. It can sit alongside grief counseling and support, but it isn't a substitute for them.

Are medium readings available in Spanish? Yes. Sessions with Danny are available in English or Spanish.

How long after a death should I wait? There's no rule. Some people come within weeks, others after years. If your grief feels very raw or overwhelming, please lean on a grief counselor or doctor as well.

Recording-setup guide (for Danny)

Keep it simple. This should feel like a session, not a production.

  • A quiet room with warm, even light — a window to the side is enough. Sit; don’t stand.
  • Two chairs at a slight angle, so it is a conversation, not an interview desk. For a solo sitting, one chair, camera at eye level.
  • One stable camera (a phone on a tripod is fine) plus a second phone for a backup close-up, if someone can start it and leave it.
  • A small lavalier or a decent USB microphone, if he has one. If not, a phone close to the speaker is better than a camera mic across the room.
  • On-screen: a short title card, his name, and the working show name. Captions in the edit.
  • Before recording: phones on silent, a glass of water, a printed run-of-show with the few lines he wants to hit, not a script to read.
